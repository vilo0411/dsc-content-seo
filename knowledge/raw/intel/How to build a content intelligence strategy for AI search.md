AI has made content easier to produce, but publishing more doesn’t necessarily help customers decide. A brand’s website, ads, social media, and service team may tell conflicting stories. Those inconsistencies can surface in AI-generated answers.

Grounding “determines which pages are retrieved, referenced, and cited,” [Microsoft explains](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/march-2026/the-ai-performance-dashboard-your-view-into-where-your-brand-appears-across-the-ai-web). When information conflicts across sources, AI responses can reflect that confusion.

That’s where content intelligence comes in. It connects customer intent, trusted information, and performance feedback to guide content creation, distribution, and updates. Done well, it provides a grounding layer for AI — a maintained source of evidence that supports accurate answers.

## 1\. Content briefs should include intent and a 360-degree view of your customer

Compare “family hotels in Miami” with a parent looking for connecting rooms, supervised kids’ activities, and late check-in. Which ages are eligible for the kids’ club? Is supervision included? Are connecting rooms guaranteed?

Understanding customer intent across the journey is critical to content intelligence. Combine search queries with CRM data, call center insights, sales conversations, reviews, surveys, paid media, and social interactions, using data with appropriate permission. These signals reveal what customers need, what influences their decisions, and where information is missing.

Use those insights to plan content around recurring questions, unmet needs, and the details that influence a customer’s choice. Build each content brief around four questions:

- What is the customer trying to accomplish?
- Which facts and constraints matter to their decision?
- What evidence can we provide?
- What should the customer be able to do next?

## 2\. Connect intent, topics, and semantics

Build topical depth around the questions that shape customer decisions. For a family hotel, that means addressing connecting rooms, kids’ club age requirements, and booking restrictions.

Connect those answers with clear semantic structure. Topic maps, internal links, and structured data help establish relevance. Depth comes from useful, distinctive answers backed by verified details and firsthand expertise. Use AI to identify what distinguishes your business, guided by what customers actually need.

[Google’s people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) rewards that originality.

## 3\. Make every passage clear on its own

Retrieval systems may pull individual passages rather than full pages, potentially losing important context.

Make each key section understandable on its own: use descriptive headings, name the product, location, or service explicitly, and keep conditions alongside the claims they qualify. Present essential information in text, even when it also appears in images.

## 4\. Use a four-step framework to assess AI visibility

The Found, Scored, Trusted, and Recommended framework helps assess AI visibility without assuming every AI platform works the same way.

| Pillar | Question | Focus areas |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Found** | Can AI systems discover and retrieve the information? | Crawlability, internal links, accessible text, and clear page structure |
| **Scored** | How well does it answer the request? | Intent alignment, topic coverage, specificity, and useful evidence |
| **Trusted** | Can it be checked and relied on? | Sources, firsthand expertise, consistent facts, ownership, and freshness |
| **Recommended** | Does the offering fit this customer? | Distinctive attributes, suitability, limitations, and proof |

Google’s guidance [for its AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) confirms established SEO practices still apply.

## 5\. Build a connected brand intelligence layer

Google’s Jason Spero [put it simply](https://business.google.com/us/think/ai-excellence/ai-and-marketing/): “AI can only be as good as the data you put into it.”

A grounding layer starts with approved product facts, policies, research, and evidence behind brand claims. These verifiable sources help AI generate accurate answers.

A context memory graph, or brand intelligence layer, is one way to connect this knowledge:

- **Context** captures the current question, circumstances, and constraints.
- **Memory** retains prior information and outcomes, within permission rules.
- **The graph** links entities, facts, content, and workflows so related knowledge can be retrieved together.

Microsoft’s [GraphRAG research](https://microsoft.github.io/graphrag/) shows how knowledge graphs help AI answer questions. Brand voice also belongs in this layer rather than in a static guide pasted into a prompt. Drafts grounded in property details, customer questions, and current policies start stronger.

You don’t need a graph on day one. A governed repository with reliable retrieval, clear sources, owners, and permissions can be enough. Brands can control grounding within their own systems, but not what third-party AI engines say.

### Use an intelligence layer to connect customer intent with consistent answers

Connecting customer signals from search, CRM, service interactions, and campaign performance with verified business information helps identify gaps, deliver consistent answers across channels, and guide updates.

For a resort’s kids’ club, recurring questions about age eligibility, supervision, schedules, costs, and reservations can be linked to verified policies. This helps the website, paid campaigns, email, social, and PR communicate consistent facts.

### Prevent content decay from creating commercial risk

Outdated offers, unanswered questions, and conflicting claims create commercial risk. Assign owners and review triggers for pricing, eligibility, and policies.

Use AI to flag gaps and draft updates, with people approving consequential claims. Track sources, AI workflows, reviewers, changes, and revalidation dates to make the process auditable.

## Measure content intelligence through business outcomes

Content volume measures activity, not impact. Evaluate content intelligence through intent coverage, accuracy, AI visibility, customer outcomes, and operational efficiency.

| **Area** | **Useful questions** |
| --- | --- |
| **Intent coverage** | Are priority customer questions answered with enough evidence? |
| **Accuracy and freshness** | How much critical information is verified, current, and consistent? |
| **AI visibility** | Is the brand present, accurate, cited, or recommended across a defined query set? |
| **Customer outcomes** | Does content drive qualified inquiries, purchases, or fewer avoidable support requests? |
| **Operational efficiency** | Can teams find, reuse, and update approved knowledge with less rework? |

Track mentions, citations, and recommendations separately. [Bing’s AI Performance dashboard](https://searchengineland.com/bing-webmaster-tools-ai-performance-report-468751) now reports citations and the grounding queries behind them.

Content intelligence connects what customers need with what a business knows. Combining customer insights with verified information helps brands deliver accurate, consistent answers across channels. Clear structure, defined ownership, and regular reviews support AI retrieval and trust.

Success ultimately depends on what content helps customers accomplish and the business achieve — not how much gets published.
